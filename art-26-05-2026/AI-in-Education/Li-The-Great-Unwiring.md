# 📉 The 'Great Unwiring': AI in Education Is Boosting Scores While Eroding Thinking — Here's What We Need to Do

![A minimalist corporate desk scene showing an open laptop beside a printed brain MRI scan, a handwritten policy document, and a steaming coffee cup, warm morning light](desk-analysis.jpg)

💡 **The uncomfortable truth is that AI in education is both working and failing at the same time.**

Two weeks ago, I sat in a curriculum meeting at a mid-sized district where a 15-year veteran English teacher shared something that stopped the room cold:

*"I asked my class to write a persuasive paragraph without using any AI tools. Three students asked if they could use Grammarly. Two asked for ChatGPT. One student looked at me and said, 'I can't read that much.' It's a paragraph. Eight sentences.*

*Something is breaking."*

That teacher isn't alone — and the data backs her up.

📊 **What the research actually shows is a paradox:**

The March 2026 meta-analysis in *Nature* (Dong Y., *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) found that generative AI generally *outperforms* traditional teaching methods in improving academic achievement and higher-order thinking. Students using AI tools scored **48% better with AI assistance** on problem-solving tasks (Bastani et al., 2025).

That sounds like a win.

🔍 **But here's the catch no one is talking about:**

The same students scored **17% worse on unassisted exams**. The ALEKS platform quasi-experiment (3.2 million student interactions) published on arXiv this month found:

→ Learning time on AI-susceptible problems dropped **26.9%** among college students and **31.3%** among high schoolers
→ A **25% cumulative decline** in the odds of correct response on retention tests for AI-susceptible content

A randomized controlled trial (N=1,222, arXiv 2604.04721) found that after just **10–15 minutes** of AI interaction, people performed worse without AI and gave up faster.

This is what the Brookings Institution called **"the Great Unwiring"** in their landmark January 2026 report — the conclusion that AI's qualitative risks (cognitive atrophy, erosion of relational trust, artificial intimacy) currently **overshadow its benefits**.

And here's the most damning part: **67% of students agree**. In a RAND survey published March 2026, two-thirds of middle school through college students said, *"The more students use AI for schoolwork, the more it will harm their critical thinking skills."*

The people using the tools are telling us they feel their thinking eroding.

🚨 **The governance gap makes everything worse:**

→ Only **6% of teachers** say their school's AI policies are clear (Stanford HAI 2026 Index)
→ 76% of UK teachers now use AI tools daily — even as 66% say they've seen critical thinking decline (NEU Survey, April 2026)
→ 95% of U.S. faculty believe GenAI will increase student overreliance (AAC&U/Elon, Nov 2025)
→ Yet 86% of education organizations continue deploying it — the highest adoption rate of any industry (Microsoft 2025)

We're flying blind at full throttle.

🌍 **But the answer isn't a ban:**

Estonia just signed a national partnership with OpenAI, rolling out a customized AI platform across all upper secondary schools. Education Minister Kristina Kallas framed it perfectly: *"The challenge is not how to keep AI out — the challenge is how to put AI into the learning process so that it accelerates and enhances cognitive growth rather than replacing thinking."*

Chandler Unified School District (Arizona) built a five-tier AI guideline system ranging from "AI Innovation" (students encouraged to use AI to enhance problem-solving) to "No AI" (specific tasks where it's banned). They limit themselves to approved platforms that protect student data.

Wellesley Public Schools (Massachusetts) created a grade-level AI spectrum: PreK–2 gets teacher-only AI use; Grades 3–5 use AI for ideation only; Middle school gets collaborative AI experimentation; High school operates on an 8-level AI use spectrum.

Columbus City Schools (Ohio) put teachers — not administrators, not tech vendors — in the driver's seat, making them the "guiding force" for per-assignment AI decisions.

These are not bans. They are **frameworks for intentional use**.

✅ **Three things every education leader should do right now:**

**1. Stop asking "Should we allow AI?" and start asking "Where does AI add cognition vs. replace it?"**
The data is clear: AI boosts performance on AI-accessible tasks and erodes it on unassessed ones. That means every assignment needs to be classified. Is this a "with-AI" task or a "without-AI" task? Be explicit.

**2. Build your AI policy on the 8-level spectrum, not a binary yes/no.**
Wellesley's model is gaining traction for a reason. AI use isn't one thing. It ranges from "teacher-only AI" to "AI for brainstorming" to "AI for drafting with human revision" to "full AI use." Different subjects, different grade levels, different assignments need different levels.

**3. Invest in teacher training before you invest in AI tools.**
85% of K-12 teachers lack formal AI training (Azumo 2026). The NBER working paper (w34851, May 2026) found that AI closed about **three-quarters of the productivity gap** between lower- and higher-education individuals — but only when combined with sustained effort and proper scaffolding. The tool alone isn't enough.

💬 **The bottom line:**

The evidence isn't contradictory — it's conditional. AI *can* enhance learning when deployed with intentionality, scaffolding, and clear boundaries. It *will* erode thinking when deployed as a replacement for student effort.

The question isn't whether AI belongs in education. It's already there — 86% adoption and climbing.

The question is whether we'll build the frameworks to deploy it intelligently, or keep flying blind.
