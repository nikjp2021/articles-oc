# Is Insider Trading Really Unfair? A Data-Driven Look at Market Impact

Last week's conviction of four individuals in the $600,000 insider trading scheme by the U.S. Department of Justice reminds us why this topic sparks such heated debate. But is insider trading always the villain we make it out to be?

Recent SEC data reveals a nuanced picture: while clear-cut cases like the December 2025 biopharmaceutical merger scheme (where executives profited over $600K from non-public acquisition information) rightly draw condemnation, academic research suggests certain forms of information sharing might actually improve market efficiency.

The 2025 Journal of Business Ethics study on congressional trading found that trades based on legislative information generated significant abnormal returns, yet notably absent prosecutions suggest our enforcement approach may be misaligned with actual market harm. When Senator trades ahead of bill enactment consistently outperform markets, are we punishing the symptom or ignoring systemic information asymmetries?

Consider the contrasting cases: Terren Peizer's 42-month sentence for exploiting 10b5-1 plans to avoid $12.5M in losses versus academic arguments that insider trading accelerates price discovery. The SEC's May 2026 charge against 21 individuals in a decade-long scheme shows clear malicious intent, but doesn't address whether all non-public information use creates equal harm.

For business leaders navigating gray areas, three principles emerge: First, distinguish between exploiting confidential corporate information (clearly unethical) versus acting on legally obtained market insights. Second, recognize that not all information asymmetry is created equal—some reflects skill, others exploitation. Third, advocate for clearer regulatory frameworks that protect legitimate information advantages while preventing true abuse.

The ethical line isn't always bright, but our response should be proportional to actual market harm rather than moral outrage alone. Where do you draw the line between unfair advantage and efficient markets?
