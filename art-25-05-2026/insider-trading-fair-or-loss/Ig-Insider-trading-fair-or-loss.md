# Is Insider Trading Fair or Foul?

Slide 1: 🤔 Is insider trading ALWAYS wrong?
Recent cases spark debate: Is it pure cheating or market efficiency?

Slide 2: 📜 What IS insider trading?
Trading stocks based on material non-public information.
Illegal when it breaches trust or confidence.

Slide 3: ⚖️ The "UNFAIR" argument
- Creates uneven playing field
- Erodes public trust in markets
- Benefits few at expense of many
- Example: Dec 2025 $600K biopharma scheme

Slide 4: ⚡ The "EFFICIENT" argument
- Speeds up price discovery
- Reflects true value faster
- Some academics argue benefits outweigh harms
- Example: Senators trading on legislative info (no prosecutions)

Slide 5: 📊 Recent cases show complexity
- Dec 2025: 4 convicted in $600K merger scheme
- Jun 2025: CEO gets 42mo for avoiding $12.5M loss
- May 2026: SEC charges 21 in decade-long scheme

Slide 6: 🔍 What determines fairness?
- Source of information (legal vs illegal)
- Breach of fiduciary duty?
- Intent to deceive vs acting on insight
- Harm caused to other investors

Slide 7: 🏛️ Regulator response
- SEC pursuing sophisticated schemes
- Focus on 10b5-1 plan abuses
- Congressional trading scrutiny increasing
- But enforcement remains inconsistent

Slide 8: 💡 For professionals: 3 guidelines
1. Know your company's specific policies
2. When unsure, DON'T trade
3. Focus on long-term value, not information edges

**Caption:**
The insider trading debate isn't black and white. While clear cases like the December 2025 $600,000 merger scheme fraud deserve punishment, academic research suggests some forms of information sharing might actually improve market efficiency.

Recent cases reveal troubling inconsistencies: executives going to prison for avoiding losses while senators consistently profit from legislative information with zero prosecutions. This raises questions about whether we're punishing the right behaviors.

For business professionals navigating these gray areas, the key lies in understanding the difference between exploiting confidential corporate information (clearly unethical) versus acting on legally obtained market insights.
