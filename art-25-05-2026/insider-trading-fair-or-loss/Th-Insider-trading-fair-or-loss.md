# Insider trading: always unfair or sometimes efficient?

Let's be real - when I hear "insider trading," I picture Wolf of Wall Street types laughing all the way to the bank. But after digging into recent cases and research, I'm questioning whether our black/white view misses important nuances.

Here's what's got me thinking:
- Dec 2025: 4 people convicted in $600K scheme using merger info (clearly wrong)
- Jun 2025: Healthcare CEO gets 42 months for avoiding $12.5M losses via 10b5-1 plans
- May 2026: SEC charges 21 people in decade-long scheme (definitely problematic)

BUT - that 2025 Business Ethics study found senators trading on legislative info consistently beat the market... with ZERO prosecutions. If acting on non-public info is always harmful, why no enforcement here?

The academic argument: insider trading might actually speed up price discovery. If bad news is coming, shouldn't prices adjust NOW rather than later when everyone finds out?

My take: It's not about whether information asymmetry exists (it always will), but HOW it's exploited. Trading on legally obtained insights? Different category than stealing board meeting minutes.

The real issue might be our enforcement focus - going after small fry while systemic advantages remain untouched.

What do you think? Is there ever a scenario where insider trading helps rather than hurts markets?

**Caption:** The insider trading debate isn't as simple as "always bad." Recent cases and research reveal important nuances about market efficiency vs. unfair advantage.
